<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Introduction: About Our Investigation</strong></h3>



<p>This report follows an extensive, months-long investigation, which included on-the-ground research at Predictive’s facilities in Utah, an undercover meeting with a key product distributor in Atlanta, and multiple conversations with former employees and industry experts. We also spoke with multiple new moms who wrongly believed they were donating their birthing tissue to purely non-profit causes such as research into how stem cells can treat deadly diseases.</p>



<p>We have investigated numerous companies and bad actors in our history, many of which have subsequently been&nbsp;<a href="https://hindenburgresearch.com/about-us/">charged by regulators with securities fraud</a>, and we can’t recall examining a business with as many issues as Predictive. We think this company is rotten to its core.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Does it Work? It is Unclear Whether the Stem Cells are Even Alive, Let Alone Functional</strong></h3>



<p>Given that Predictive’s stem cell products have not been FDA approved or cleared, they are offered only&nbsp;<em>as FDA registered tissue products</em>. What this means is that it was registered using an online form through the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cber/CFApps/Login/Index.cfm">FDA’s website</a>, without the thorough independent vetting of an FDA testing process.</p>



<p>At one point, Predictive apparently portrayed that its product had a large number of viable stem cells based on a report from a University of Utah lab. However, it turned out that Predictive had&nbsp;<a href="https://regenexxusa.wpengine.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/Corecyte-side-by-side-comparison.pdf">altered the report</a>&nbsp;to include conclusions about the number of viable cells which were never actually in the original.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/18.png" alt="" class="wp-image-460"></figure>



<p>A competitor did an&nbsp;<a href="https://www.regenexx.com/blog/will-ever-find-live-mscs-another-cord-product-bites-dust/">analysis</a>&nbsp;of the&nbsp;<em>unaltered</em>&nbsp;lab work, declaring that “these cells have been through the proverbial wringer.” He couldn’t conclude that there were&nbsp;<em>any</em>&nbsp;live stem cells in the company’s products.</p>



<p>Doubts about the live cell count of stem cell products are not exclusive to Predictive.&nbsp;<a href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/news-desk/the-birth-tissue-profiteers">ProPublica</a>recently wrote an investigative piece on aggressive profiteering in the world of stem cells. The piece found that products often had little evidence to back up claims of efficacy and even that many had no live cells at all.</p>



<p>The ProPublica article focused largely on the Utah Cord Bank, a competing manufacturer of Predictive which processes similar types of stem cell product. (In fact, Predictive’s&nbsp;<a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/doug-schmid-89b45915/">Chief Laboratory officer</a>&nbsp;was one of 2 key employees at the Utah Cord Bank before&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/B6M5120QgucCDZ">defecting to Predictive</a>).</p>



<p>Per the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.newyorker.com/news/news-desk/the-birth-tissue-profiteers">ProPublica article</a>:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>For most of these products, there’s not many healthy cells left,” Daniel Kuebler, the dean of the School of Natural and Applied Sciences at Franciscan University of Steubenville, Ohio, said</p><p>…Even if there are some cells that are still alive post-thaw, “I have a hard time getting them to grow,” Kuebler said. “Just because they’re alive doesn’t mean they’re not in the process of dying.</p><p>Lisa Fortier, a researcher at Cornell University and a consultant for a company that sells birth-tissue products,&nbsp;<a href="https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/5982402-Fortier-Proteomic-Analysis-and-Cell-Viability-of.html">tested</a>&nbsp;nine (products) from four manufacturers—[Predictive was not one of them]—and also found no live cells. <p>A&nbsp;<a href="http:/inceptiondx.com/">web capture of the InceptionDX</a>&nbsp;website from August 6<sup>th</sup>&nbsp;2018, just weeks prior to Predictive’s acquisition, shows that it was just an undeveloped template. The website consisted of nothing more than boilerplate Latin ‘placeholder’ text that is common on brand new, undeveloped website templates. Example from the web capture:</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/37.png" alt="" class="wp-image-480"></figure>



<p>The&nbsp;<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/2018/09/predictive-technology-group-acquires-inception-dx/">press release</a>&nbsp;announcing the acquisition was thin on details, but seemed to focus on the importance of having a CLIA lab.</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/38.png" alt="" class="wp-image-481"></figure>



<p>Instead of buying an actual CLIA lab, the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/ex105.htm">acquisition agreement</a>&nbsp;shows that Inception DX only had “an initial CLIA registration and CLIA lab protocols based on modifications from those used by Taueret Laboratories.”</p>



<p>This was odd for 2 reasons:</p>



<ol class="wp-block-list"><li>Predictive had already paid for CLIA lab protocols through an earlier deal. In July 2016, 2 years prior to the Inception DX acquisition, Predictive&nbsp;<a href="https://backend.otcmarkets.com/otcapi/company/financial-report/171938/content">paid 300,000 shares</a>&nbsp;to unspecified recipients for “protocols related to the operation and management of a CLIA lab and related genetic tests.”</li><li>Predictive ended up&nbsp;<a href="https://www.marketwatch.com/press-release/predictive-technology-group-completes-acquisition-of-taueret-laboratories-2019-03-26-8184043">acquiring Taueret</a>, the company that the CLIA protocols were based on, just months later. This acquisition included a CLIA lab, rendering the CLIA protocols purchased from Inception DX seemingly useless.</li></ol>



<p>We find it suspicious that Predictive paid unnamed recipients&nbsp;<em>twice&nbsp;</em>for mere CLIA lab protocols<em>,&nbsp;</em>only to end up buying the very lab that the CLIA protocols were based on months later anyway.</p>



<p>The remaining “trade secrets” detailed in the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/ex105.htm">purchase agreement</a>&nbsp;consisted of “Gen DB records- birth, death, or marriage records for over 31,900,000 individuals.” This asset also looks to have been moved into Inception DX from Taueret Laboratories, given that the database was&nbsp;<a href="http:/taueret.com/Taueret_Laboratories/Taueret_Laboratories_GenDB.html">proprietary to Taueret Laboratories</a>&nbsp;prior to the formation of Inception DX.</p>



<p>As noted above, the Inception DX deal also included 400,000 shares of Juneau BioSciences. Inception DX acquired these shares on the exact date of the acquisition as part of an ‘unrelated’&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/ex102.htm">agreement</a>struck between Predictive and Juneau.</p>



<p>That agreement called for Juneau to use Inception DX’s CLIA lab in exchange for an immediate payment of 400,000 shares to Inception DX. Again, Inception didn’t have a CLIA-certified lab, nor ever ended up getting one as far as we can tell.</p>



<p>In other words, Predictive negotiated a deal with Juneau to have Juneau give Inception DX 400,000 shares of Juneau to use a lab that didn’t exist, and then acquired Inception DX later that day, using the 400,000 shares as a partial rationale for the acquisition. (To pose an obvious question: why wouldn’t Predictive just have negotiated to be paid the Juneau shares directly?)</p>



<p>Our suspicions are punctuated by the fact that&nbsp;<strong>every one of these</strong>&nbsp;<strong>entities (Predictive, Inception DX, Juneau, and Taueret) shared the same address</strong>: 2749 Parleys Way, Salt Lake City, Utah. Talk about circular dealing!</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Inception DX Acquisition: 90% of the Purchase Price Went to Unnamed Individuals in the Caymans</strong></h3>



<p>This all begs the question, why would Predictive pay over $14 million for a newly-formed entity with only &nbsp;cash and lab equipment worth $1.5 million, and &nbsp;some assets apparently shuffled in from other entities based out of Predictive’s same address?</p>



<p>Deal records show that 90% of the acquisition value went to an entity in the Caymans with unnamed owners.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Juneau BioSciences Acquisition: A Tiny Company with Deep Connections to Predictive’s CEO And His Brother</strong></h3>



<p>Next let’s turn to Juneau Biosciences, a company that develops genetic tests related to women’s healthcare. [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181819000124/pred04182019form10a.htm">Pg. 48</a>] In particular, Juneau has been developing a test for endometriosis called the ArtGuide, which it hopes to commercialize this year.</p>



<p>Juneau was formed in 2006 and is a small company with limited financials. The 2017 balance sheet reported total assets of just $183,190 and liabilities of $2.647 million. [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg. 35</a>]</p>



<p>Predictive’s filings include Juneau’s 2016 and 2017 financials, which show that all revenue was related-party. The company reported a loss in each year: [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg 36</a>]</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/40.png" alt="" class="wp-image-483"></figure>

<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>The Company’s lack of liquidity and recurring operating losses raise substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern.</p></blockquote>



<p>We contacted a former employee&nbsp;who worked with a related company via LinkedIn, asking to speak as part of our due-diligence. He said that Juneau had about 5-6 dedicated employees by 2013, focused primarily on endometriosis research. By all measures, it was a modest organization.</p>



<p>Despite its small size, the company seems to have been significantly influenced by Predictive CEO/President Brad Robinson and his brother Eric Robinson.</p>



<p>Per Brad Robinson’s bio we see that he previously served as the VP of Business Development for Juneau starting in 2010, and “was responsible for developing strategic partnerships and the company’s capitalization.” [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg. 43</a>]</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Juneau BioSciences Acquisition: Predictive Has Paid Over $50 Million For Its Minority Ownership in Juneau, and is Expected to Make Another $12 Million in Cash Payments By 2021</strong></h3>



<p>Despite its modest size and apparent precarious financial state, Predictive has directed a tremendous amount of money toward Juneau, starting as early as 2013. [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg. F-18</a>] Since that time, we calculate that Predictive has paid over $22 million in cash and stock to purchase shares from unnamed shareholders and debtholders of Juneau and to support Juneau’s various obligations.</p>



<p>Predictive has also added to its Juneau stake by acquiring 2 other companies that held shares of Juneau, including a ~$30 million purchase of LifeCode Genetics, an entity whose apparent sole asset was a stake in Juneau.</p>

The company is slated to pay roughly $12 million in monthly cash payments between now and 2021 in order to purchase additional Juneau shares. [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181819000029/ex105.htm">Pg. 1</a>]</p>

[<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/ex101.htm">Pg. 8</a>]</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Juneau BioSciences: Did Predictive Buy A Potential Game Changer in Endometriosis Diagnostics Testing – or a Dud?</strong></h3>



<p>So, what has Predictive purchased using all that cash and stock?</p>



<p>Juneau is hoping that its ArtGuide test, which is currently in development, will revolutionize diagnostic testing for endometriosis, a painful disorder that affects millions of women and can be associated with infertility. Currently, there is no blood test that can identify endometriosis through DNA biomarkers.</p>



<p>The ArtGuide test would be considered a Laboratory Developed Test which will not undergo FDA testing. This will make reimbursement a challenge, leaving the company to target self-pay markets [<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg. 9</a>&nbsp;&amp;&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181818000241/pred12042018form10.htm">Pg. 6</a>].</p>



<p>In October 2018, Predictive&nbsp;<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/2018/10/predictive-diagnostics-launches-endometriosis-risk-test/">announced</a>&nbsp;that it is beta testing its ArtGuide test and hopes to launch it in Q2 of this year. Despite these forward moving signs, industry experts and the company’s own missed historical milestones give us strong doubts about whether this latest announcement truly represents a leap forward toward rapid commercialization.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Juneau BioSciences: Proposed Endometriosis Test Already Being Met with Industry Skepticism</strong></h3>



<p>When we saw Juneau’s&nbsp;<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/2018/10/predictive-diagnostics-launches-endometriosis-risk-test/">October announcement</a>&nbsp;about the ArtGuide test’s imminent commercialization pathway, we reached out to several endometriosis experts to get their take. They were&nbsp;<em>unanimously</em>&nbsp;skeptical.</p>



<p>This is what the CEO of the World Endometriosis Society (operators of&nbsp;<a href="http://endometriosis.org/">Endometriosis.org</a>) told us:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>We did see the press release from Juneau BioSciences last year.</p><p>However, there has been no peer reviewed publication in the scientific literature about this ‘discovery’, its methodology, population(s) tested, sensitivity/specificity of the test, types of endometriosis detected, etc.</p><p>Nor is there any evidence/basis to the claim that ‘27 million women in the United States are symptomatic of endometriosis’.</p><p>This joint statement from the World Endometriosis Society and the World Endometriosis Research Foundation remains status quo.</p></blockquote>



<p>The ‘joint statement’ is referring to a March 2018 a&nbsp;<a href="https://endometriosisfoundation.org/news/article/diagnostic-biomarkers-for-endometriosis/">consensus statement</a>&nbsp;put out by both the World Endometriosis Research Foundation (WERF) and the World Endometriosis Society (<a href="https://seekingalpha.com/symbol/WES">WES</a>), two of the largest endometriosis research organizations. The statement looks to have been aimed at debunking a slew of claims by various proprietors to have ‘solved’ the endometriosis biomarker conundrum (the very type of claim being made by Juneau). From the statement:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p><strong>To date there are no data that definitively support any claims of a validated diagnostic test</strong>&nbsp;–&nbsp;<strong>or any combinations of tests – to accurately diagnose endometriosis or provide monitoring of disease progression/regression</strong>… There are presently no endometriosis diagnostic or treatment biomarkers that have been demonstrated to have the validity and reliability necessary to be used in routine clinical diagnosis or care.</p></blockquote>



<p>We also contacted the Endometriosis Research Center (<a href="http://www.endocenter.org/">endocenter.org</a>) and received this response:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>Thanks for reaching out to the ERC. We are aware of blood tests for diagnosing endometriosis.&nbsp;<strong>Although they provide a good step towards learning more about this disease, there are concerns.</strong>&nbsp;We continue to monitor the research.</p></blockquote>



<p>The Feinstein Center for Medical research wrote the following to us:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>The short answer is due to the complexity of the genetics involved in endometriosis (based on VERY large studies) –&nbsp;<strong>it seems unlikely genetics alone will successfully diagnose this complex condition</strong>.</p></blockquote>

<p>The boiler room operator was also&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/zGaivvYHg5cIld">charged criminally and pled guilty</a>.</p>

He was&nbsp;<a href="https://www.ilsos.gov/adminactionssearch/adminactionssearch?command=viewPDF&amp;itemId=92%203%20ICM7%20PRODCMZ13%20SE_AA_MgtView59%2026%20A1001001A14E13A90514D7403018%20A14E13A90514D740301%2014%201051">charged by the Illinois Securities Department</a>&nbsp;however, for similar allegations of securities fraud in relation to selling of penny stocks. Price ultimately&nbsp;<a href="https://www.ilsos.gov/adminactionssearch/adminactionssearch?command=viewPDF&amp;itemId=92%203%20ICM7%20PRODCMZ13%20SE_AA_MgtView59%2026%20A1001001A15A13A95843D1208718%20A15A13A95843D120871%2014%201051">settled those charges</a>.</p>



<p>We find it immensely disconcerting that the vast majority of Predictive shares were controlled by individuals alleged by various regulators to have been engaged in pump and dump schemes, issuing false press releases, or boiler room sales, including Predictive’s Chairman.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Predictive’s CEO/President Bradley C. Robinson Left Some Things Off His Official Biography, Including Allegations of Securities Fraud and Allegations of Non-Compliant Marketing of a Medical Product</strong></h3>



<p>Beyond its key backers, we explored Predictive CEO/President Bradley Robinson’s track record in more detail and discovered several red flags and omissions.</p>



<p>Around 2010, Robinson led a company called&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/bnfyTqrbxPxbuJ">Ceptazyme</a>&nbsp;(and a related company called Zus Health) which sought to market an algae water product grown in distilled water that the company claimed could lower cholesterol.</p>



<p>In an&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1101026/000107878213001206/f8k061313_ex1z1.htm">investor lawsuit</a>, Robinson&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/6t5ISDhuTJgTc">was alleged</a>&nbsp;to have engaged in securities fraud relating to the business. The complaint alleged that Robinson made grandiose claims about the product’s efficacy and claimed that it would be pitched by Dr. Oz, the Gates Foundation, and others. None of the claims ever materialized. Per a&nbsp;<a href="https://www.courthousenews.com/algae-water-hey/">local news article</a>&nbsp;that covered the story:</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/48.png" alt="" class="wp-image-491"></figure>



<p>We presume the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/6t5ISDhuTJgTc">lawsuit</a>&nbsp;settled, as the last&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/Ak1JVqp9cJRnjB">court document</a>&nbsp;we found indicated that a resolution was pending.</p>



<p>The algae company had also struck a partnership with a public company to market the product, but the deal quickly fell apart, with the public company also&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1101026/000107878213001206/f8k061313_8k.htm">suing Robinson</a>&nbsp;over allegations that his company:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow"><p>Failed to market the Registrant’s product in a manner compliant with state and federal regulations, and allowed its distributors to make claims and representations that were not in compliance with applicable regulations, among many other breaches.</p></blockquote>



<p>Those allegations strike us as extremely prescient given Predictive’s current business.</p>



<p>The charges between the two companies&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1101026/000107878213001206/f8k061313_ex1z1.htm">were ultimately settled</a>&nbsp;without any admission of wrongdoing.</p>



<p>Aside from the Ceptazyme debacle, Robinson also left his role at ActiveCare off his official biography. ActiveCare was a public company focused on diabetes management. Robinson and his brother Eric&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1429896/000121390017003262/fs12017a6_activecare.htm">served at ActiveCare</a>&nbsp;as Director and CFO/General Counsel/Director respectively. They&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1429896/000121390017013222/f8k120617_activecareinc.htm">resigned simultaneously</a>&nbsp;about 8 months before the company&nbsp;<a href="https://www.csbankruptcyblog.com/wp-content/blogs.dir/375/files/sites/55/2018/07/petitions-for-relief.pdf">declared bankruptcy</a>&nbsp;in 2018.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>All in the Family: The CEO’s Brother Represents a Construction Company Hired By Predictive</strong></h3>



<p>Speaking of brotherly affiliations, Predictive CEO/President Brad Robinson and his brother Eric have been involved in multiple business interests. As we noted earlier, both Brad and Eric had key roles a Juneau, a company that Predictive has had deep business ties with. Both brothers also had brief roles at public company ActiveCare (which went bankrupt), and both were involved in Specialized Health Products, Inc., led by their uncle and Father.</p>



<p>When we see family members that regularly engage in business with one another, especially when those businesses overlap with public company interests, we look for potential undisclosed related-party transactions.</p>



<p>Eric Robinson is an attorney by trade. A check of his&nbsp;<a href="https://services.utahbar.org/Member-Directory/Profile?customercd=6641">Utah bar profile</a>&nbsp;shows that he currently works at Eckman &amp; Mitchell, a construction firm. On the Eckman Construction website, we see that an&nbsp;<a href="https://eckmanconst.com/portfolio/healthcare/">upcoming project is planned</a>&nbsp;for…Predictive Biotech</p>



<figure class="wp-block-image"><img decoding="async" src="https://hindenburgresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/49.png" alt="" class="wp-image-492"></figure>



<p>We find it rather odd that Robinson’s diverse interests — ranging from construction to laboratory science — overlap with a public company run by his brother. Nonetheless, if Eckman has launched this project (or does in the future) we hope the company will disclose the compensation paid on this deal.</p>



<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Predictive’s CEO/President Bradley C. Robinson: Official Biography Doesn’t Tell the Full Story</strong></h3>



<p>Much like the details&nbsp;<em>omitted</em>&nbsp;from CEO Brad Robinson’s official bio, the items&nbsp;<em>included</em>&nbsp;also raise questions. For instance, here are several claims that we have taken the “shine” off of and “un-spun”:</p>



<p><strong>Claim:&nbsp;</strong>Robinson’s&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1382943/000109181819000165/pred05182019form10a2.htm">official biography</a>&nbsp;states he “was the CEO and co-founder of Infusive Technologies, LLC from November, 2004 until September, 2008 when it was acquired by Sagent Pharmaceuticals, Inc.”</p>



<p><strong>Un-Spun:&nbsp;</strong>Sagent&nbsp;<a href="https://www.fiercebiotech.com/biotech/sagent-pharmaceuticals-inc-acquires-rights-to-multi-chamber-syringe-technology-and-launches">acquired</a>&nbsp;Infusive’s key assets relating to a new syringe technology (<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/797564/000136086511000012/hst8kjan272011.txt">not the whole company</a>.) Sagent paid $1.25 million up-front for the technology, then was slated to pay additional amounts upon achievements of certain milestones, including upon receipt of an FDA 510K device approval.</p>



<p>The Sagent deal ended in litigation, however. According to a&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/FpeD5a0HwJObiY">2011 lawsuit</a>&nbsp;filed by Robinson’s entity, Sagent failed to advance the commercialization of the technology.</p>



<p>Sagent&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/4igtyXrS5xH2hN">countersued</a>, detailing how the device was rejected by the FDA with a deficiency letter. Sagent alleged that Robinson botched the 510k application, and that he also concealed material facts from Sagent including &nbsp;knowledge that former employees did not properly assign their patents to Infusive, and &nbsp;knowledge that international patent applications had been botched as well.</p>



<p>The parties ultimately&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/yGSgl7K1KwtN29">settled</a>&nbsp;on undisclosed terms. Robinson’s entity, Infusive, was apparently allowed to&nbsp;<em>keep</em>&nbsp;the FDA-rejected syringe technology, because Infusive later took the technology public via&nbsp;<a href="https://www.biospace.com/article/releases/-b-global-holdings-inc-gbhd-b-acquires-b-infusive-technologies-llc-b-and-its-chasyr-multi-chamber-syringe-technology-/">reverse-merger</a>&nbsp;in the entity that ultimately became Predictive! [<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/PRED-June-30-2017-Financial-Statements-FINAL1.pdf">Pg. 20</a>]</p>



<p>(Merle Ferguson, the SEC-alleged securities fraudster who became Predictive’s Chairman, was&nbsp;<a href="https://www.biospace.com/article/releases/-b-global-holdings-inc-gbhd-b-acquires-b-infusive-technologies-llc-b-and-its-chasyr-multi-chamber-syringe-technology-/">CEO</a>&nbsp;of the entity at the time in 2014.)</p>



<p><strong>Claim:</strong>&nbsp;Robinson’s&nbsp;<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/2018/03/brad-robinson-full-profile/">official biography</a>&nbsp;states that he “studied accounting at the University of Utah and received a Masters of International Management from the Thunderbird”.</p>



<p><strong>Un-Spun:</strong>&nbsp;This is true, but while Robinson&nbsp;<em>studied</em>&nbsp;at the University of Utah, he&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/2cG4i9iVelOMml">never actually graduated</a>. He later received an&nbsp;<a href="https://www.slideshare.net/secret/khWBk0e2Tn9hVB"><em>executive</em>&nbsp;MBA</a>&nbsp;at Thunderbird, which doesn’t require an undergraduate degree. A Thunderbird admissions officer told us that the undergraduate degree requirement can be “surpassed by the quality and quantity of professional work experience a student had at that time.”</p>



<p><strong>Claim:&nbsp;</strong>Robinson’s&nbsp;<a href="http://predtechgroup.com/2018/03/brad-robinson-full-profile/">official biography</a>&nbsp;states that he was a “Director/Co-Founder of Specialized Health Products, Inc. (“SHPI”), acquired by C.R. Bard, Inc.”</p>



<p><strong>Un-Spun:&nbsp;</strong>This is also true, but SHPI was actually run by Robinson’s uncle, who served as Chairman and CEO. Robinson’s Dad served as the CFO.&nbsp;<a href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/790228/0000931731-96-000189.txt">SEC filings</a>&nbsp;show that Robinson was around 24 years old when he joined, an apparent fresh college drop-out at the time, yet was given a board seat at the family business.</p>



<p>And that pretty much brings us full circle.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading"><strong>Predictive’s Auditor: Have You Ever Heard of BF Borgers? Neither Had We</strong></h3>

If you’ve never heard of BF Borgers, we won’t hold it against you – neither had we. You are unlikely to find their clients anywhere on the S&amp;P 500 (or any major national exchange for that matter).</p>



<p>Most of&nbsp;<a href="https://rasr.pcaobus.org/Firms/FirmSummaryPublic.aspx?FirmID=F0C298206D055E20355165A6436401FA">BF Borgers’ clients</a>&nbsp;are sub $20 million market cap companies that are listed on the pink sheets, including esteemed firms such as:</p>
